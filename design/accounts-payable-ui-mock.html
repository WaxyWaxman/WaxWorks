Layout mock, not working code. All three modals go away: Record payment and Clear selected become the right track's reading of the selection, and Create new becomes its third state. What M-05 now says

The layout needs no decisions. Three tracks, a retractable slab and a sticky right column are how this app already works — E-05 d29 by way of E-02 d38, E-07 d17, M-01 d17, and most recently M-02 d31 for Order Processing. Folding the three modals into track 3 changes no behaviour: Record payment, Clear selected and Create new do exactly what d4, d5, d12–d16 already say, in a place that does not cover the ledger they are about.

Seven rows were appended, and one of them reverses a considered decision. d18 supersedes d11, which says in as many words that a claim credit “isn't earmarked to one Invoice the Manager picks” — that was not a misreading or a slip, it was deliberate, and types.ts:257 encodes it. The rest extend rather than reverse: the remit-to address, the one-action settlement, partial credit (which d18 forces), settled Invoices staying readable, and payment terms in two other flows. All seven are written in — M-05 d17–d21, M-01 d19, E-02 d45 — with d11 struck through in place and the prose it contradicted corrected rather than left to drift.

What this is worth. Not tidiness. Today the Manager ticks rows and then guesses which of two buttons will light up, with the reason hidden in a title attribute. Here the right track reads the selection back — what it sums to, which action it is legal for, and why the other one is not. The failure this prevents is the one M-05 d15 is most exposed to: Clear is manual reconciliation with no undo described anywhere in the flow, and a selection that does not sum to zero should be refused out loud rather than silently.

Recorded rowWording
M-05 d17 recorded The Supplier's billing address is displayed on the accounts payable screen as the remit-to address, alongside the payment being recorded. Extends the M-01 d13 line inherited into this flow, which captures a billing address and notes that nothing here consumes it. Recording a payment is the moment the store asks “where does the cheque go”, and the answer is already on file one screen away. It is display only: nothing is printed, addressed, or sent, and M-01 remains the only place the address is edited. Accepted consequence: a stale billing address now looks authoritative at the moment it is most likely to be acted on, and nothing on this screen can tell that it is stale — the Supplier card is still the only place it is maintained.
M-05 d18 supersedes d11 The Manager chooses which outstanding Invoices a Credited claim's amount is applied to, and how much lands on each. Supersedes decision 11 in full: the automatic oldest-received-first distribution is retired, not kept as a default. d11 was itself a clarification of d6–d7, and reversing it restores d7's plain reading — a claim is applied against Invoices from the same supplier, now with the Manager saying which. Why the reversal is cheap: the model already stores credits per Invoice — architecture.md:184 derives an Invoice's balance from ap_payments plus credited SupplierClaims, and Invoice.creditsApplied exists. d11 constrained who decides the split, never whether a split could be recorded; this is a policy and UI change, not a schema change. Accepted consequence: every credit application is now a decision, including the trivial one where there is a single outstanding Invoice and no judgement to exercise. The screen does not pre-fill a suggested split, because a pre-filled split is d11 wearing a disguise — and one nobody would check.
M-05 d19 recorded A claim credit and a payment may settle the same Invoices in one action, producing one PaymentBatch whose targets carry which kind each was. Extends decisions 6 and 16, and contradicts neither: d6 already says a credit “settles a balance the same way a payment does”, and d16 already says one Record Payment produces one batch covering “whatever mix of Invoices and manual entries it paid”. That a credit could be one of the things in the mix was never addressed either way — the two being separate acts was an omission, not a position. The Manager settles $68.65 as $12.40 of credit and $56.25 by cheque, once, and the batch says so. Accepted consequence: a batch can now have a zero money half — a credit-only settlement — for which no payment reference exists, so the reference stops being unconditionally required and becomes required only when money actually moves.
M-05 d20 forced by d18 A Credited claim's amount may be spent in parts, across several settlements, until it is exhausted. Resolves the “Partial claim credit” open question, which this flow already carried. It is not optional: once d18 lets the Manager say how much lands on each Invoice, “applied once, in full” cannot survive — a credit larger than the Invoices picked has a remainder, and d11's rule for absorbing it on the last one went with d11. The claim therefore carries an amount spent and a remaining, and leaves the list only when remaining reaches zero. Accepted consequence: a claim credit can now sit part-spent indefinitely, which is a new way for money in the store's favour to be quietly forgotten — the reason the right track's top slot reports the remaining figure rather than the original.
M-05 d21 recorded A settled Invoice remains readable on this screen, in a band separate from the outstanding list, and names the PaymentBatch or batches that settled it. Extends step 5 and decision 16. Step 5 says a fully-paid Invoice “leaves the outstanding list for payment history” — it names where the Invoice goes, not that it stops being visible, and this honours it by putting it somewhere that is explicitly not the outstanding list. d16 made history browsable in one direction only (a batch, opened to see its targets); the reverse — an Invoice, saying what paid it — was unaddressed. Accepted consequence: the ledger grows without bound, so the band is collapsed by default and carries a count rather than rows.
M-05 d22 recorded A PaymentBatch can be voided, whole, by a Manager — never edited and never deleted. The void is appended against the original, which stays in the ledger as the record of what was recorded at the time (E-02 d23's pattern; the lexicon reserves delete for drafts and void for finalized artifacts). It reverses every target: money returns to the balance owing, claim credit returns to that claim's remaining — d20 is what makes that expressible, since without a partial model a credit could only be returned in full. Whole or not at all, because d16 made the batch the unit of the act. Accepted consequence, and the significant one: an Invoice taken back out of Paid returns to Finalized and becomes correctable again, so immutability (E-02 d40, A-33) means immutable while paid, not immutable forever. A-33 needs the amendment appended.
M-01 d19 recorded A Supplier carries payment terms — Net 15 / 30 / 45 / 60, On receipt, COD, or Prepaid — as the default terms for Invoices received from them. New field, constraining nothing existing. It is not Cancel-by, which this card already carries and which is an ordering figure: days from order-placed to auto-cancel an unfulfilled order. Nothing on this card, or anywhere else, recorded when a bill is due before this. Accepted consequence: Prepaid and COD are not aging terms and the field quietly admits two different kinds of thing. A Prepaid Supplier that nevertheless carries an outstanding balance is either a data-entry error or a real exception, and that is left open in M-05 rather than answered here — the mock surfaces it as an anomaly rather than choosing.
E-02 d45 recorded An Invoice carries its own payment terms, defaulted from the Supplier and overridable at receiving, and a due date derived as invoice date + terms. Extends decision 2, which captures both the invoice date and the received date but says nothing about which governs payment timing. Net-N runs from the invoice date: the supplier's clock starts when they bill, and the due date has to agree with the statement it will be reconciled against. The Invoice's own terms win over the Supplier's, because the paperwork in hand is the agreement. Accepted consequence: stock that sat in transit arrives with part of its window already spent, and a slow enough shipment can be received already overdue — correct, and the screen should show it that way rather than granting a grace period from the received date.

What each track is for

Left — the payables slab (retractable)
The store-wide figure at the top, then the lookup, then the chips, then the sort, then who is owed. Two controls that do different jobs and look different: chips filter the list (their counts are suppliers), a supplier row scopes the middle to that supplier's ledger. The lookup is not a filter of the list — it reaches any Supplier including one with no payables at all, which is step 1's explicit requirement and the only way to start a Create new against a supplier you have never owed. Shut, a 52px strip carries Create new, a credits badge, and the suppliers tiled by their short code.
Middle — the combined ledger
Decision 3's one list — Invoices, Claims and manual entries together, because what is owed is the net of both. A real table rather than On Order's row grid: six money columns that have to line up is what this screen is for. Each row is a selection, not a navigation.
Right — the selection (sticky)
Three states, in strict priority: the new entry you are composing, else the selection you have ticked, else the supplier's standing. All three modals collapse into it.

Why the right track is the selection

On Order's track 3 is the line; Customers' is the customer. The equivalent here is not one row — it is however many rows you have ticked, because every action in M-05 acts on a set. A payment covers a mix (d16); clearing needs at least two (d15); applying a credit spreads across all of them (d11).

So the track reads the set back and names the one action it is legal for. Ticked invoices → per-target amounts, method, reference, date. Ticked entries summing to zero → clear, with d15's “this changes nothing about the balance” said where you can read it. Anything else → a red block that says the sum and what is wrong with it. The two disabled buttons and their tooltips go away.

The bands, and the ones still not drawn

On Order bands its list on d19's overdue grouping. The equivalent line through a payables ledger is d14: a Claim placeholder does not move the balance until it is cleared against a matching Credit. Today that is a word in a Status column; here it is a band, and the figures above and below it mean different things. d21 adds a fourth — settled Invoices, collapsed, naming what paid them.

There are still no aging bands, now for a better reason. The column is here, because terms make a due date computable. The buckets are not, because M-05 words its open question as one about aging's home — here, or a general reporting surface — and 30/60/90 drawn on this screen answers that by drawing it. A per-row fact and a report are different things, and only the first is a layout decision.

Why aging was stuck, and on what

M-05's aging question reads as though it were a reporting choice. It is not: nothing anywhere records when a bill is due. The Supplier's complete field table (M-01:22–40) has no payment terms, and the field that looks like it — Cancel-by (M-01:31) — is explicitly “days from order-placed to auto-cancel if unfulfilled”, an ordering figure. The Invoice carries an invoice date and a received date (E-02 d2) and no due date.

So “30/60/90+ days overdue” — M-05's own words — could not be computed at all. Days-since-invoice could, and that is what the first pass of this mock showed; but days outstanding is not days overdue, and presenting one as the other is the kind of plausible figure that gets acted on. The open question should say it is blocked on a field two other flows own — which is why the proposal above lands in M-01 and E-02 before it lands here.

Two things the terms decision must settle rather than assume, both visible on screen: net-N runs from the invoice date, so a slow shipment can be received already overdue; and Prepaid and COD are not aging terms at all — Crate Digger is Prepaid and owes $42.00 anyway, which the right track flags as an anomaly instead of quietly rendering a due date for it.

One settlement, two tenders

Superseding d11 is the interesting half, but the half that removes the most friction is d19. Before it, settling an Invoice partly by credit was two acts producing two artifacts — apply the credit, then record a payment for whatever was left — and the ledger showed no sign they were the same event.

Now a target row has two fields, credit and money, and the foot names the split before you commit: $12.40 credit + $56.25 cheque. One PaymentBatch carries both, tagged, so opening it later says what actually settled the invoice. Nothing about d16 had to change — it already said one batch covers “whatever mix” it paid.

One thing that is constrained, and stays so: a manual Credit entry is not allocatable in a settlement. d14 says it reduces the balance the moment it is entered, so it has already netted at the supplier level; letting it also be spent against a picked Invoice would count it twice. Only claim credit — which d11 held back precisely because it had not yet landed — is allocatable.

Voiding, and what it costs

A payment recorded in error is voided (d22), never edited and never deleted — the house pattern for reversing anything finalized (E-02 d23). The batch stays in history, struck through, as the record of what was recorded at the time.

Whole or not at all. d16 made the PaymentBatch the unit of the act — one cheque covering four Invoices is one thing that happened. Voiding one target out of it would leave a batch describing a settlement that never took place in that shape, which is the thing d16 exists to prevent. A partly-wrong payment is voided and re-recorded.

The expensive part is not the money. Reversing balances is arithmetic; d20's partial model already lets the claim credit go back where it came from, which is why the mock records which claim funded each credit target rather than drawing from an anonymous pool. What is expensive is that an Invoice can now stop being Paid — and immutability attaches at paid (E-02 d40, A-33). So a frozen document can be unfrozen, and immutable has quietly come to mean immutable while paid. The confirmation names the invoices this will unfreeze before you press it, rather than reporting it afterwards.

The alternative was worse: an Invoice left locked while carrying a balance it owes, with nothing able to correct the error the void exists to correct. A-33 needs an amendment appended — that is an architecture decision, not this branch's to write.

The round trip back to Receiving

Every Invoice row carries a link to its receiving document, and the verb changes rather than the link disappearing: Open in Receiving while it is correctable, View in Receiving once it is paid.

This needed no decision at all — it is an affordance for a round trip four documents already describe. E-02 d4 and d40, architecture A-33 and the lexicon's entry for immutable all say the same thing: a finalized Invoice stays correctable until a Manager marks it paid here, and marking it paid is what freezes it. Accounts payable is one end of that round trip and had no way to walk it.

Manual entries carry no such link. They are not sourced from Receiving (d12), and accounts payable never creates a Receiving Invoice (d9) — so there is nothing at the other end.

A thing the bands turned up

In the screen as it stands the ledger's Balance column does not add up to the owed badge above it, and it cannot, because the column means two different things depending on the row.

A Credited-but-unapplied Supplier Claim renders as -amount at screens/AccountsPayable.tsx:140, and a Claim placeholder renders its signed face value at screens/AccountsPayable.tsx:149 — but the net owed is computed from invoiceBalance plus payableEntryContribution, and neither of those rows contributes to it (lib/totals.ts:150, :194). The figures are each defensible on their own; the column is not, because it mixes “what this contributes” with “what this is worth for clearing”.

The code is right and the layout was hiding it. M-05 step 6 has the Manager mark a claim Credited and then apply it, as two acts; d14 keeps a placeholder out of the balance entirely. Three bands, each with its own subtotal, make the table sum to the headline again — which is why this mock draws them, and why the fix is layout rather than arithmetic. Not a decision: a reading of d6, d11 and d14 that the current screen does not make visible.

Why gift cards moved into the slab

Today the registry is a full-width table under the supplier grid, which reads as an afterthought bolted to a screen about suppliers. But d10 puts it here for a reason that is structural: it is money owed, and so is everything else in the slab. Making it the last destination in that list — under a rule, tiled in black so it never reads as a Supplier — says what d10 means without adding a word to the flow.

Selecting it scopes the middle track to the registry and the right track to the total liability. Nothing about it is editable here: loading and redeeming happen at the till (E-05), and this is the register, not the mechanism.

Two totals, not one

The slab's store-wide figure is one line per currency, not a sum. M-05 requires a Supplier's currency to carry through, with the store-currency equivalent shown at the configured rate — and that rate lives in M-06, which is not built. Adding CAD 282.45 to USD 23.75 would be inventing an exchange rate in the one place on the screen most likely to be read as authoritative.

When M-06 lands, this becomes a single figure with the per-currency lines beneath it. Until then the honest shape is the one that cannot be wrong. The same restraint applies inside a foreign-currency supplier's ledger, where the caveat sits in track 3 rather than a fabricated conversion sitting in the table.

The credit in hand

The top slot of track 3 — where On Order puts who is waiting — here holds claim credit nobody has applied. Same reasoning: of everything true about this supplier, that is the one thing that is money on the table and the only one a person has to go and do. It reports the remaining figure, not the original, because d20 lets a credit sit part-applied indefinitely — a new way for money in the store's favour to be quietly forgotten.

An earlier version of this mock previewed where the credit would land — which Invoices, how much on each — because d11 made that computable. d18 retired the algorithm, so there is nothing to preview: the Manager types the split. The band's job narrowed to saying how much is in hand and taking you to the settlement, and no suggested split is pre-filled, because a pre-filled split is d11 wearing a disguise and one nobody would check.

What is mock data

The prototype's seed carries two open Invoices, one Credited claim and one Adjustment — enough to prove the store works, not enough to show a layout. This mock adds, for display only: a second F.A.B. invoice carrying a partial payment (so d4 has something to show), a Claim placeholder and the Credit that matches it (so d14's band and d15's clearing have rows), and one consignment Supplier, Vinyl Revival, so M-01's consignment flag and d13's type default are visible.

None of that is a proposal about seed data. When this is built, the screen should render whatever the store holds, including the empty cases — which is why the settled supplier, the zero-balance card, and the reachable-but-inactive Walk-in / trade-in are all still in here.

Open, and deliberately left open

  • A cross-supplier view. On Order's slab has a permanent Everything on order first row. This one has no equivalent, on purpose. A combined ledger implies a payment run across suppliers, and both d7 (a claim applies only within one supplier) and d16's PaymentBatch are supplier-scoped as written. Building the view first and discovering the model second is the wrong order. Recorded as an open question, not designed around.
  • Aging buckets. The column is answered; where the 30/60/90 report lives is not, and this screen does not decide it.
  • Overpayment. Typing more than an Invoice's balance is refused here, but M-05 defines no behaviour for it at all — a supplier overpaid by accident is a credit in the store's favour that nothing models. Refusing is the mock's choice, not the flow's.
  • Undoing a clear, and now undoing a settlement. d15 does not say whether a clearing can be reversed, and d18–d20 add a second irreversible act: a credit spent against the wrong Invoice. Neither is offered and neither is promised. Worth raising before d18 is ratified — automatic distribution had no wrong answer to undo.
  • Prepaid and COD. Whether a Prepaid Supplier can carry a balance at all is behaviour M-01 d19 has to answer. The mock shows the anomaly and declines to resolve it.
  • Terms on a manual entry. A hand-entered Invoice or Consignment defaults to the Supplier's terms here, which is a proposal inside a proposal — d12 says a manual entry is a lump not sourced from Receiving, and whether it should inherit terms at all is undecided.
  • Who the reference belongs to. d5 makes the payment reference free text because the bank statement is the reconciliation surface. Whether the screen should warn on a duplicate reference within one supplier is not decided, and is left alone.
  • Currency movement. An invoice raised at one rate and paid at another is M-05's own open question. The composer records the amount in the supplier's currency and says nothing more.
  • PRD §4 drift, found while checking this. PRD:119 still defines APPayment as “a payment recorded against a supplier Invoice” — singular and Invoice-scoped. M-05 d16 replaced that with PaymentBatch and §4 never received the amendment. Not this branch's to fix; it belongs in /spec-audit.
