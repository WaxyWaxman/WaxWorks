A · Ledger
Data-first
The store is a book of accounts. Tables everywhere, hairline rules, tabular figures, no cards.
Receiving. Around 35 lines visible without scrolling; the eye runs straight down a column.
Nothing is presentable. Cost and margin sit in plain sight, and a new hire faces a wall of numbers.

B · Counter
Task-first
One screen, one job. Huge scan field, big blocks, very few things visible at once.
Point of Sale. A 3–6 line sale never needs density, so the screen buys clarity instead.
Receiving becomes a scroll marathon — 100 lines at this row height is roughly eight screens.

C · Crate
Content-first
The sleeve is the interface. Cover art leads every row; warm paper ground, editorial type.
Search. Staff recognise a sleeve faster than a catalogue number, and it faces a customer well.
Art costs vertical space, and A-14 stores a URL — a MusicBrainz miss is a row of empty squares.

D · Console
Command-first
One always-focused input takes a barcode, a search term or a command. Screens are its output.
Speed ceiling. E-05 four barcode types and E-03 search collapse into a single field.
Half the crew's work is mouse-driven form filling. Commands do nothing for them.

E · Workbench
Structure-first
One skeleton on every screen: list left, detail centre, a pinned context rail on the right.
Consistency. Learn it once, and the reconcile panel stays pinned while 100 lines scroll past it.
Three columns eat the width the receiving table needs, and you pay for simultaneity you said you don't need.

They are not mutually exclusive. A and B could be one product — Ledger density in the back, Counter clarity at the till — if you accept two idioms in one app. That is a real choice, not a fudge; it is what “genuinely both” on input already implies.
The ratings are judgement, not measurement. They come from the row heights drawn here against a 50–100 line invoice and a 3–6 line sale, not from testing. Nothing on this canvas is a recorded decision.
