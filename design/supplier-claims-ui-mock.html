One track, four acts

Track 3 follows what track 2 has selected: a batch shows the send, a sent claim shows its three endings. Only Mark Credited is the primary orange — Abandon and Void are plain, because one brings money in and the other two admit that none is.

The amber box is the number worth designing around. E-04 d20 exists because what a supplier grants routinely differs from what was claimed, and says the memo’s amount is recorded alongside the claim total rather than replacing it. Nothing on today’s screen shows that gap, so the store absorbs a few dollars a claim with no surface that ever says so.

The grey box is the same idea for the other outcome. An abandoned claim is a write-off, and writing it down is what makes the decision visible later — which is the whole argument M-05 d27 makes for retiring a placeholder out loud rather than letting it happen quietly.

Where revision 2 landed

Written 1 · Unsent, derived from the sent date

“Unsent” replaces the code’s invented Draft and is derived — but from the sent date (d23), not from the absent claim number as revision 2 had it. d10 designs the number to be typed by a person, and hanging a state on a hand-edited field is a hazard by construction. The sent date is written by the system in the same act and nobody has a reason to edit it.

That became E-04 d25, superseding d21’s derivation while leaving its principle alone, and A-43 as the general rule: a derived state keys on a fact the system writes, never on one a person may edit. It completes A-35 and A-33b, which said what to derive without ever saying from what.

Settled 2 · The batch is the unit, and it is standing

A raised line drops into its supplier’s batch — the base one, or a separator-exclusive one where the line carries a separator. Sending a batch turns it into one numbered claim. Nobody picks lines at send time; the separator already did.

So the unsent band draws batches, not tickable rows, and this screen has no multi-select at all in that band. That is a real simplification over revision 1, which had borrowed M-05 d27’s ticking for a problem that does not have M-05’s shape.

Written as E-04 d22, which also settles where the letter comes from: it is chosen as the claim is raised and blank by default, not inherited from the PurchaseOrder the copy arrived on. That letter splits outgoing streams, and by the time a copy is in hand the stream that carried it has been sent and closed — reusing it would batch claims by an accident of ordering history rather than by how the store wants to argue them.

Settled 3 · The address was already decided — my note was wrong

Revision 1 said a claims address was “not yet a field.” It is. M-01 d13 already carries a shipping address and already designates it — “where stock ships from — the address a claim is argued against” M-01:40, with the same words repeated in the type types.ts:35–41. The email field is likewise recorded as “where orders/claims are sent” M-01:38.

So there is nothing to decide and nothing to add. The send panel now shows both, labelled so the difference from the billing address — where cheques go, M-01 d17 — is visible at the moment it matters.

My answer 4 · Days waiting — yes, and it is nearly free

You asked whether we should care. Yes, for one reason that only became true in this revision: with Abandon on the screen, waiting time is the thing that prompts the decision. Without it nobody ever decides — a claim just sits, which is exactly the orphan you were pointing at.

And it is cheap. The number is assigned on send; writing sentAt in that same moment is one more column on a write that already happens. Contrast M-05’s aging, which needed a payment-terms concept in two other flows before anything could be computed at all.

What I would not build: aging buckets. A per-row figure, one sort and one chip is the whole affordance — the same line the Payable mock drew, for the same reason: a per-row fact and a report are different things, and only the first is a layout decision.

Written as E-04 d23 — and it turned out to carry more weight than an affordance. Because the sent date is written by the system, it is also the right thing to derive sent-ness from, which is what d25 and A-43 do. The column bought a figure and a foundation for one write.

New 5 · Abandon — the disposal claims never had

Your suggestion, and it fills a hole the record already half-admits. E-04’s open question covers a supplier who denies a claim E-04:151 and offers nothing; meanwhile M-05 d27 already retires a Claim placeholder by including it in a settlement, and justifies it in words that fit here exactly — “a Manager deciding to stop chasing it … a real disposal, and the screen should say so rather than letting it happen quietly.” An E-04 claim has no equivalent.

Two things worth being precise about, because they change how big this is:

• It is not a money orphan. M-05 d26 keeps a Pending claim outside the balance, so an unanswered claim was never inflating what A/P thinks is owed. It is a visual orphan — a row that never leaves. That makes Abandon a tidying act, not a correction, which is the right expectation to set.

• It does not touch stock. The copies left on-hand when they were adjusted out under d3/d4. Abandoning stops the store expecting money and does nothing else — worth stating in the decision, because “write it off” reads like an inventory act and is not one.

Written as E-04 d24, manager-only following d3 — writing off money owed is the same standing as a hard on-hand adjustment. Accepted consequence recorded with it: raising a claim is an Employee act and disposing of a dead one is not, so an Employee can create work only a Manager can clear. A queue one role fills and another drains backs up unless somebody looks — which is what d23’s days-waiting figure exists to make visible.

Written 6 · Void, and why it is not Abandon

The number is ours, not theirs — the supplier answers with their own credit memo reference, which d20 makes the point of truth — so a wrong number costs nothing and uniqueness is the only property it has to hold (d26). It is frozen on send, and a claim that is wrong is voided and raised again rather than edited (d27).

That gives the screen two endings that both keep the row and mean opposite things, so they are drawn apart and labelled: abandon is a loss, void is a mistake. A-44 records why they are built differently too — abandoned is a status the row carries, voided is a row in supplier_claim_voids, because a voided_at column would be an update to the row the void exists to leave alone.

The cost, recorded: “is this claim finished” becomes a status read or a join, and a query that remembers only the first counts voided claims as live.

Done 7 · Numbering and slab order

The CLM- prefix is gone — claim numbers are plain ascending integers, and the column header says Claim so the row does not have to. d10 is untouched: still ascending, still unique, still overwritable where a supplier insists on their own reference.

The slab now bands Unsent first, then waiting, then closed, and the default sort is supplier name A–Z. Worth noting this is the reverse of M-01 d17’s reasoning and agrees with its conclusion: A–Z is a lookup, and bands across a lookup split the name you want into two places — but here the bands are a work queue (send these, then chase those), which is what the screen is for, so they earn their place under any sort.

Layout Carried over unchanged

Not carried over: Payable’s .ap-ledger table. A claim is a few lines with prose in them — a reason and a note — rather than six money columns that must align, so track 2 is a list opening to short tables.

Still open What is not answered

A supplier who issues a credit note against a different invoice than the one claimed E-04:151. Untouched by anything above, and not a layout question — it is about what a credit memo is allowed to reference.

Whether a claim raised in error but never sent needs anything at all. d27 voids a sent claim; an unsent line has no number, no sent date and no artifact in the world, so removing it from its batch ought to be an ordinary edit — but nothing says so.
